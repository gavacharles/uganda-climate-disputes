**When a risk-allocation clause becomes a dispute generator**

Every weather clause in a construction contract is meant to do one thing: allocate a risk in advance, so the parties don't have to fight about it later.

FIDIC does this with "exceptionally adverse climatic conditions" (8.4(c) in 1999, 8.5(c) in 2017). The logic is sound. The Contractor prices normal weather, the Employer carries the exceptional, and an extension of time follows when the line is crossed.

But the clause never draws the line. It doesn't say:

- which climatic variable counts (rain only? heat?);
- which data source decides;
- over how many years "normal" is measured;
- whether the test runs month by month, season by season, or over any period the claimant picks.

The FIDIC Contracts Guide offers a rule of thumb (a return period of 4–5 times the Time for Completion), not a contractual test.

So the decisive questions are answered after the event, by the party with the most to gain from each answer.

I tested this on Uganda's road contracts with 45 years of daily climate data:

1. In nearly 9 out of 10 arguable claim seasons, a different but defensible choice of dataset or baseline reverses the verdict.
2. Letting the claimant choose the assessment window after the event almost doubles the share of contracts with "exceptional" weather.
3. Uganda's own numerical formula (MoWT Clause 1229) does not escape the problem. It delegates the data to "records… or as agreed to by the Engineer". In practice I found the averages tables, adopted at tender, did not match the site climate. The same clause could produce hundreds of days of extension, or none.
4. Heat is a "climatic condition" too, and already a third of weather-lost days in Uganda. No contract measures it.

A clause meant to prevent disputes has become a source of them. The cause isn't bad faith: it's a definition the contract leaves open.

The fix is drafting, not litigation. Name the dataset and location in the Contract Data. Attach the monthly "normal" tables at tender. Fix the window. Share the excess in bands, which is what I call a Shared Weather Allowance. And say what happens to "normal" as the climate shifts.
